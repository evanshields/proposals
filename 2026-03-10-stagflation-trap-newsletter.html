The Stagflation Trap

The Fed Just Lost the Game. Here's What It Means for Your Deals.

Issue #6 March 10, 2026 1,642 words ~7 min read

Weekly insights from the folks building it.

Good Monday afternoon.

This week, two numbers dropped that changed the macro picture entirely. The February jobs report came in at negative 92,000 - against expectations of roughly positive 150,000. That's a 250,000 miss. Unemployment ticked up to 4.4%.

Meanwhile, the Iran conflict is pushing oil toward $120/barrel. A geopolitical shock colliding with job softness. That's the definition of stagflation: weak growth, reaccelerating inflation, and no clear policy response.

I'm going to walk you through what this means operationally for anyone with a deal in their pipeline.

1. Big Idea

The Fed's Impossible Choice: Stagflation Is Here

The February jobs report and the Iran conflict just created the scenario the Federal Reserve has been desperately trying to avoid. And there's no policy move that doesn't hurt.

Here's the macro setup:

The Jobs Miss: The U.S. economy lost 92,000 jobs in February. Year-over-year, job growth fell 88% compared to 2025. That's not a slowdown. That's a contraction. The unemployment rate hit 4.4%, up from 4.1% in January.

The Oil Shock: Iran escalated tensions in the Strait of Hormuz this week. Oil is trading near $120/barrel. Every $10 increase in the price of oil costs an additional $50-100 per unit in construction labor and materials. If you're building multifamily housing, energy costs ripple through everything: steel, concrete, transportation, diesel for equipment.

The Trap: The Federal Reserve has one job (actually two): keep employment strong and inflation low. But those two directives are now pulling in opposite directions.

  • If the Fed cuts rates to stimulate jobs: Oil is expensive, inflation reaccelerates, and you're back to 2022 problems.
  • If the Fed holds rates steady: The economy keeps losing jobs, unemployment rises, and you're looking at recession.
  • If the Fed raises rates: You're deliberately choking off economic growth to fight inflation. Explicit recession.

This is stagflation. Weak growth. Rising prices. No exit.

Why This Matters Right Now

Most underwriting models built in the last 18 months assume:

  • Stable (or falling) unemployment
  • Interest rates holding around 4-4.5%
  • Oil trading in the $70-80 range
  • Cost inflation moderating from 2022-23 peaks

None of that is true anymore.

If you underwrote a 150-unit project six months ago assuming $85/barrel oil, $280/sq ft construction costs, and a 3.5% cost inflation factor - you're underwriting a deal that no longer exists.

Here's what's changed in your pro forma:

  1. Material costs spiking again. Oil at $120 vs. the $85 you modeled? That's another $50K-100K per unit. For a 150-unit development, you're talking $7.5M-15M in unanticipated cost.
  2. Labor uncertainty. February jobs loss doesn't mean unemployment rises uniformly. Construction in some markets may get cheaper (less competition for workers). But skilled trades are still tight. Expect wage pressure.
  3. Financing cost uncertainty. Mortgage rates just crossed below 6% for the first time since 2022. But that's because of rate-cut expectations. If the Fed doesn't cut (or hikes), rates could move back to 6.5-7%. Your construction financing and takeout loan assumptions need stress-tested.
  4. Buyer frozen paradox. Here's the weird part: mortgage rates below 6% should unlock demand. But buyers aren't moving. The lock-in effect is structural, not rate-driven. Buyers are priced out from the 2021-era rate environment. Lower rates don't create buying power if prices don't fall. For rental housing, this is actually positive (apartments benefit from weak home sales), but it's a timing gamble.

What Smart Operators Are Doing

In a stagflation scenario, you can't forecast your way out. But you can de-risk through operational discipline:

  1. Lock in material pricing NOW. If oil stays elevated, steel and concrete prices will follow. Long-term supplier contracts (9-12 months if you can negotiate them) are worth the premium. You're buying certainty.
  2. Extend hold timelines. If you underwrote a 5-7 year hold expecting 3% annual appreciation, you're in trouble. Stagflation doesn't produce price growth. It produces cap rate expansion (values compress). Plan for a 7-10 year hold. If conditions improve and you sell early, that's a bonus.
  3. Right-size equity contributions. If your development pro forma has a 15% equity contribution assumption, stress-test it to 20-25%. A stagflation environment will require more equity and more patience.
  4. Pick operators with fortress balance sheets. In weak growth, weak operators die. Strong operators acquire their assets. If you're choosing between a deal with a new sponsor and one with a seasoned operator, choose the operator. They'll survive the cycle.
  5. Operate geographically smart. Some Southeast markets have built-in inflation hedges: low property taxes (vs. California), stable construction labor supply (vs. shortage-tight Northeast), and rent growth driven by population inflows (not speculation). Florida, Georgia, and North Carolina are more resilient than coastal high-cost markets because they have structural housing shortages. That short doesn't go away in stagflation.

The Bigger Picture

Stagflation is the housing crisis accelerant. When growth is weak and inflation is rising, governments cut the knees out from housing supply (tariffs, regulatory delays, anti-development sentiment). When growth is weak, private developers pull back on ambitious projects. The 4 million home deficit we covered last week doesn't get better in stagflation. It gets worse.

But that's also where you come in.

Stagflation is brutal for speculative developers. It's lethal for operators with weak balance sheets. But it's a competitive advantage for disciplined institutional capital with a 7-10 year horizon. That's the environment we're building in right now.

The economy just gave us a permission structure to do what we should be doing anyway: lock in long-term value, right-size underwriting, and build for a cycle, not a quick flip.

2. Signal vs. Noise

Three stories satisfying your need to know. Two worth acting on. One worth ignoring.

✓ Signal: The NLIHC Gap Report - 30 Affordable Units Per 100 Extremely Low Income Households

The National Low Income Housing Coalition just released their annual GAP Report. The headline: there are only 30 affordable apartments available for every 100 extremely low income (ELI) households in America. No state has adequate supply.

What does that mean? If you earn 30% of area median income or less, you're mathematically priced out in every state. For a family of three in Alabama earning $16,000/year, an affordable two-bedroom apartment costs $400/month. The actual median rent? $1,150. The gap is structural, not cyclical.

This is your scoreboard. Every LIHTC deal you close, every workforce housing unit you build, every deed restriction you negotiate is movement on this number. In the next 5 years, we need to build roughly 170,000 LIHTC units annually just to move the needle 10%. The market is currently producing 110,000.

Why it matters: This stat is the intellectual bedrock of Built Different. It's the reason affordable housing is a $20B/year addressable market. It's the reason LIHTC rents can sustain cap rates above market multifamily. This gap doesn't close without operators like you.

Go deeper: National Low Income Housing Coalition - GAP Report

✓ Signal: Data Centers Are Attracting Capital Away From Housing

HighBrook Capital just closed a $266M fund dedicated to data center real estate. Galvanize raised $370M for decarbonized real estate. And Sound Point closed their third private credit fund at $1.5B.

Here's what's happening: Capital is rotating out of traditional residential development. The reason isn't hard to find. Data center yields (10-12%+ unlevered) beat multifamily (4-6% cap rates). Decarbonization projects have government incentives built in. Private credit funds have shorter timelines and less regulatory friction.

For you, this means: competition for your LPs is fiercer. The conversation with family offices and institutions now includes "Why your deal instead of a data center fund?" The answer has to be clear: mission alignment, demographic tailwinds (population growth in your market), and a housing shortage that compounds annually.

Why it matters: You're not competing for capital just against other housing operators. You're competing against every alternative asset class with better short-term returns. Your edge is long-term structural scarcity + mission. Lean into both.

Go deeper: Axios Pro Rata coverage of alternative real estate investment flows

✓ Signal: Shirley Chisholm and the Housing Rights We Take for Granted

Shirley Chisholm (1924-2005) was the first Black woman elected to Congress. She served on the House Education and Labor Committee and fought for fair housing enforcement, food stamps, early childhood education, and the foundational infrastructure that lets working people stay housed.

Her quote: "I am, was, and always will be a catalyst for change."

Here's why this matters in 2026: Chisholm didn't just talk about housing discrimination. She built the legal and political scaffolding that made fair housing enforcement possible. The Fair Housing Act existed on paper in 1968. But enforcement - actually holding lenders and landlords accountable - came through people like Chisholm pushing federal agencies to act.

Today, 25% of renters are rent-burdened (paying >30% of income on housing). Among renters of color, that number is 40%. The policy infrastructure Chisholm fought for is the reason we have enforcement mechanisms at all. Without them, we'd be back to redlining and racial discrimination as de facto housing policy.

Why it matters: Building 10,000 affordable units operates inside a legal and political framework that didn't exist 50 years ago. That framework didn't appear by accident. It was built by activists, legislators, and advocates like Chisholm who understood that housing is a rights issue, not just a market issue.

3. Built in Public

We're Stress-Testing a 2,000-Unit Pipeline Through Stagflation Right Now

Last week I mentioned we're stress-testing a 2,000-unit pipeline. This stagflation setup makes that operational discipline urgent, not optional.

Here's what we're changing in our underwriting:

  • Material price locks: We're negotiating longer-term supply contracts (9-12 months out) to protect against oil-driven commodity spikes. Yes, we pay a premium upfront. But certainty in a stagflation environment is worth it.
  • Hold timeline extensions: Projects we initially modeled as 5-7 year holds are now 7-10 year holds. Stagflation doesn't reward quick exits. It rewards patient capital with fortress balance sheets.
  • Equity contribution stress tests: We're running scenarios where our equity contribution bumps from 15% to 22%. If the market moves against us, we want to survive. If it moves with us, we've over-reserved and our returns are even better.
  • Geographic specificity: We're doubling down on markets with structural housing shortages and favorable tax environments. The Southeast - Florida, Georgia, North Carolina - is where we're concentrating. Population inflows + low property taxes + reasonable regulatory timelines = resilience in stagflation.

This isn't pessimism. This is clarity. Stagflation is the environment we're going to operate in for the next 2-3 years. The operators who thrive are the ones who build for that reality, not the ones hoping conditions improve.

4. Numbers

$1.3B+ in Unanticipated Insurance Costs: Florida's Multifamily Crisis Is Rewriting Every Deal's Math

Florida insurance costs have tripled in many markets in the last 24 months. What was a $2,000/unit annual insurance expense in 2022 is now $4,500+/unit in 2026. For a 150-unit multifamily property, that's an additional $375K/year in operating costs.

Why? Citizens Property Insurance Corporation - Florida's insurer of last resort - shed 180,000 policies last year and now holds less than 10% market share. Private insurers fled. Less competition = pricing spikes. Insurance is now 15-25% of total operating expenses in some Florida markets.

Here's what this means operationally: Every Florida deal underwritten before 2024 without a hard insurance quote (not just an estimate) is sitting on catastrophic ROI risk. A property that pencils at a 7% cap rate with $2K insurance suddenly trades at 5.5% cap rate with $4.5K insurance. That's the difference between a deal that works and one that doesn't.

Smart operators are doing two things:

  1. Re-underwriting existing deals with current insurance actuals. Some won't survive that pencil.
  2. Negotiating pre-commitment insurance agreements before breaking ground on new development. You want a locked rate from a carrier, not a quote that changes month-to-month.

The gap between a $2K/unit assumption and a $4.5K/unit reality isn't a rounding error. It's a capital allocation decision. It affects return, hold timeline, and exit pricing.

5. Indelible Impact: Women's History Month

Shirley Chisholm (1924-2005)

Unbought. Unbossed. And the first to fight for working people's right to housing.

In 1968, Chisholm became the first Black woman elected to the U.S. House of Representatives. She ran on a platform of "Unbought and Unbossed." What people remember less is that she spent her 14 years in Congress building the regulatory infrastructure that made fair housing enforcement possible.

She served on the House Education and Labor Committee and authored or co-authored legislation on:

  • Fair Housing enforcement (pushing federal agencies to actually prosecute discrimination)
  • Food stamps and nutrition programs (because housing insecurity is tied to food insecurity)
  • Early childhood education (because working parents can't afford to leave the workforce to manage housing crises)

Her connection to housing: Chisholm understood what most policy advocates miss: housing isn't an isolated issue. It's downstream of employment, education, healthcare, and credit access. You can't house a family that's food insecure. You can't stabilize a community where schools are underfunded. You can't enforce fair housing if lenders and landlords face no consequence.

She fought for the infrastructure that lets working people stay housed. That infrastructure - fair lending laws, enforcement mechanisms, Section 8 vouchers, the framework for affordable housing development - is the operating environment for every deal we build.

Why now: Stagflation will make housing more expensive and scarce. The political pressure to cut corners on fair housing enforcement will spike. Chisholm's legacy is a reminder that housing is a rights issue first, a market issue second. Building differently means building equitably.
